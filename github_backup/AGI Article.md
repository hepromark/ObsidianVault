The author's main argument and critique revolve around the dangerous ideology behind the rhetoric of "superhuman AI" and its misleading implications for understanding human intelligence and value.

**Main Argument:** Shannon Vallor, a Chair in the Ethics of Data and Artificial Intelligence, argues that the widespread description of generative AI systems like ChatGPT and Gemini as "superhuman" is **not merely marketing spin but a dangerous and powerful ideology**. This ideology, whether used to promote unquestioning enthusiasm or fear, fosters a growing **devaluation of human agency and autonomy** and collapses the distinction between conscious human minds and the mechanical tools we create. She contends that this rhetoric ultimately **obscures and erases the most vital dimensions of humanity**.

**Core Critique:** Vallor's critique is multifaceted:

• **AI's Fundamental Lacks:** She emphasizes that today's powerful AI systems fundamentally lack essential features of human minds. They do not possess **consciousness or sentience**, nor the capacity to feel emotions like pain, joy, fear, or love. They also lack any sense of their place or role in the world, or the ability to experience it. AI tools are described as "dark inside," serving primarily to answer questions, generate images, or create deepfake videos. They are "mindless producers of efficiencies".

• **Misleading Definition of "Superhuman":** Vallor challenges the definition of "superhuman AI" used by researchers like Yoshua Bengio (outperforming humans on a "vast array of tasks"). She argues that the word "superhuman" implies "human, but more so"—possessing the same human powers to a greater degree or additional powers, rather than being something of a radically different kind that lacks fundamental human qualities but performs better on certain metrics. For example, we don't call airplanes "superhuman" even though they travel faster than humans. The fictional Superman, in contrast, embodies and exceeds human ideals, possessing all fundamental dimensions of the human personality, including desires, suffering, love, grief, hope, care, and doubt, experienced even more intensely.

• **Reduction of Human Intelligence and Value:**

 Vallor critiques the shift in the definition of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI). Traditionally, AGI aimed for intelligence "equal to humans," including self-aware consciousness, problem-solving, learning, and planning, making it "indistinguishable from the human mind".

 However, she notes that entities like OpenAI, Geoffrey Hinton, and Yoshua Bengio have redefined AGI as "highly autonomous systems that outperform humans at most **economically valuable work**". This "bait-and-switch" **erases anything not considered economically valuable work from the definition of intelligence**, leading to a massive reduction of human capacity and self-understanding.

• **Historical Devaluation:** Vallor points out that attempts to erase and devalue the humane parts of human existence are not new, dating back to the Industrial Age, where humans were often viewed as flawed, inefficient, fungible machines for productive output. AI, she argues, is merely the latest excuse for this reductive and cynical ideology. She highlights that this struggle has been hard-fought by labor and human rights movements defending non-mechanical, non-economic standards for human valuation like dignity, justice, autonomy, and respect.

Ultimately, Vallor argues that by describing an "entirely insensible and unthinking" entity as "superhuman," we implicitly devalue the concept of being human and all that humans can do and strive to become. She hopes that the "moral and experiential poverty of AI" might paradoxically lead to a cultural reclamation and restoration of our most vitally human dimensions and values.
